## What makes a photo biometric?

A photo is biometric when it can be used for automatic face recognition. A computer compares the face in your passport photo with your face, at an e-gate or by a border officer's system, so the photo must show your face clearly, straight on, at a known size and in even light.

ICAO defines a biometric passport, or eMRTD, as a passport, visa or card "that has a contactless integrated circuit embedded in it and the capability of being used for biometric identification of the MRTD holder." Facial recognition is the one biometric every eMRTD must support; fingerprints and iris are optional. That is why the photo matters so much:

- **United States:** since 2007, US passports have had a chip that stores "a biometric identifier in the form of a digital image of the passport photograph," according to US Customs and Border Protection.
- **United Kingdom:** "All UK passports use facial biometrics to measure facial features," and "Passport photographs must meet the required standards to be used for biometrics," says HM Passport Office.
- **European Union:** EU passports must carry a chip that contains a facial image and fingerprints, under Regulation (EC) No 2252/2004.

You do not need a separate "biometric" photo for an e-passport. A photo that meets your country's passport rules is a biometric photo.

## The ICAO standard explained

ICAO publishes the rules for travel documents in Doc 9303, and it set out detailed photo guidance in a 2018 technical report on portrait quality. Countries base their own rules on it, and they usually make them stricter.

### Face position

- **Straight on.** ICAO allows very little turn or tilt: no more than about 5 degrees up, down or sideways, and 8 degrees of roll (tilting your head towards a shoulder).
- **Head size.** In the technical report, the head from chin to crown takes up between 60% and 90% of the image height, and the head's width between 50% and 75% of the image width. Countries set much narrower ranges, so use your country's numbers.
- **Detail.** For a digital photo, the report asks for at least 90 pixels between the centres of the eyes, and gives 120 pixels as best practice. For a printed photo, Doc 9303 recommends at least 10 mm between the eyes.

### Expression

ICAO's guidance asks for a neutral expression, both eyes open and the mouth closed. Some countries are a little more relaxed: the US State Department's online renewal guidance allows "a neutral facial expression or natural smile," though not unusual expressions or squinting. When in doubt, keep a neutral face with your mouth closed.

### Lighting and background

- **Background:** "The background shall have a uniform colour." Countries choose the colour: white or off-white for the US, light grey or cream for the UK, white for India.
- **Lighting:** even, with no "extreme dark shadow visible on the face," and no red eye.
- **Glasses:** if allowed at all, the frames must not cover the eyes and there must be no glare over them. The US does not allow glasses except for medical reasons.
- **Head coverings:** for religious reasons they must not hide any facial features or cast shadows.

ICAO is now moving the way face images are stored on the chip to a newer standard, ISO/IEC 39794-5, by 2030. This changes how the data is encoded, not what a good photo looks like.

## Biometric vs regular passport photo

Today a regular passport photo and a biometric one are the same thing: if it meets the passport rules, it meets the biometric standard. The real difference is between a biometric photo and an ordinary portrait:

| | Biometric photo | Ordinary portrait |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Pose | Straight on, head level | Any angle |
| Expression | Neutral, eyes open, mouth closed (some countries allow a slight smile) | Any |
| Background | Plain, uniform, light | Anything |
| Lighting | Even, no shadows, no red eye | Shadows for effect are fine |
| Head size | Set range inside the frame | Free |
| Editing | No retouching, filters or AI changes | Retouching is common |

The full list of what one country checks is in our [passport photo rules checklist](/tool/passport-photo-requirements).

## Biometric photo size

There is no single biometric photo size. Doc 9303 recommends that submitted photos "should be 45.0 mm x 35.0 mm," and many countries use that 35 × 45 mm frame, but each country sets its own:

| Country or document | Print size |
| :--- | :--- |
| UK, Ireland, Schengen countries, India | 35 × 45 mm |
| United States | 2 × 2 in (51 × 51 mm) |
| Canada | 50 × 70 mm |
| China | 33 × 48 mm |

Our [guide to the 35 × 45 mm format](/tool/35x45mm-passport-photo) covers the most common size, and the [list of photo sizes for every country](/tool/passport-photo-size-by-country) covers the rest. For a Schengen visa, see our [guide to the photo size for a Schengen visa](/tool/schengen-eu-photo-size).

Germany shows where things are heading. Since May 1, 2025, paper photos are no longer accepted for German ID cards and passports applied for in Germany: the photo is taken at the authority, or by a registered photo service that sends it to the authority electronically. German missions abroad still accept 45 × 35 mm paper photos until they have their own photo equipment.

## Biometric photo near me

Anywhere that takes passport photos takes biometric photos: pharmacies, post offices, shipping stores, photo studios and, in some countries, photo booths. Ask for the photo for your country and document by name, for example "a UK passport photo" or "a Schengen visa photo", rather than just "a biometric photo", so you get the right size. Our [guide to places that take passport photos](/tool/passport-photos-near-me) lists the main options, and our [guide to visa photo services nearby](/tool/visa-photo-near-me) covers visa sizes.

A **biometrics appointment** is something different. If US Citizenship and Immigration Services asks you to attend one, it takes your photo and fingerprints there; you do not bring a biometric photo. Our [guide to USCIS photo rules](/tool/uscis-immigration-photos) explains which forms still need printed photos.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is a biometric photo the same as a passport photo?

For almost every country, yes. Passports today are biometric, so a photo that meets your country's passport rules is a biometric photo. The term matters mostly when you compare it with an ordinary portrait or selfie.

### What size is a biometric photo?

It depends on the country. ICAO recommends 35 × 45 mm, which the UK, Schengen countries and India use. The US uses 2 × 2 inches and Canada 50 × 70 mm.

### Can I smile in a biometric photo?

ICAO's guidance asks for a neutral expression with the mouth closed, and most countries follow it. The US also accepts a natural smile with the mouth closed. A big smile showing teeth is refused almost everywhere.

### Can I take a biometric photo at home?

For many countries, yes, including the US, as long as someone else takes it and it meets every rule. Some countries, such as Canada, require a commercial photographer, and Germany no longer accepts paper photos for applications made in Germany.

### What is an ICAO standard photo?

A photo that meets the portrait guidance of ICAO, the UN agency that sets travel document standards: face straight on and evenly lit, neutral expression, eyes open, plain background and no retouching. National passport rules are based on it.
