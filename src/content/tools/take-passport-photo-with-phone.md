## Can you take a passport photo with your phone?

Yes, if it meets the rules. The State Department's online renewal guidance says most phones take photos in an acceptable size and format. What it rejects is a poor phone photo: its photo FAQ lists "low quality vending machine or mobile phone photos" among those that are not acceptable, and its passport rules say not to change a photo "using computer software, phone apps or filters."

So a phone works well, as long as the picture is sharp, evenly lit, taken from the right distance and left unedited.

## Camera settings

Change these before you take the photo. Menu names vary by model and software version.

### iPhone

- **Use Photo mode, not Portrait mode.** Portrait mode blurs and alters the background, which is an edit.
- **Use the 1x or 2x lens, not 0.5x.** The ultra-wide lens stretches faces, especially near the edges.
- **Turn off filters and keep the photographic style neutral.** Anything that changes skin tone or colour counts as a filter.
- **Turn the flash off.** Flash causes glare and harsh shadows; use window light instead.
- **Leave the file format as it is.** iPhones usually save photos as HEIC, which the online renewal system accepts.

### Android

- **Use the standard photo mode**, not portrait, "live focus" or background-blur modes.
- **Turn off beauty, face retouch or skin-smoothing features.** Some phones switch these on by default for the front camera.
- **Use the main (1x) lens** or a slight zoom, not the ultra-wide lens.
- **Turn off scene or AI enhancement** if your phone has it, and turn off filters.
- **Turn the flash off.**

## Selfie vs having someone take it

**Have someone take it if you can.** The State Department's guidance is to have someone take your photo, with you several feet from the camera and your full face visible. A selfie at arm's length puts the camera too close, which makes the nose look bigger and the ears smaller, and it is hard to keep the phone level.

If you are on your own:

1. Put the phone on a tripod or a stack of books at eye level, a few feet away.
2. Use the rear camera, which is usually sharper than the front one.
3. Set the self-timer (3 or 10 seconds), or use a voice or palm-gesture shutter if your phone has one.
4. Take a test shot, check the framing, and adjust before the final photos.

## How to take a passport photo with your phone, step by step

1. **Change the settings** above: standard photo mode, 1x or 2x lens, no filters, flash off.
2. **Stand in front of a plain white or off-white wall** in even daylight, facing a window, with nothing else behind you.
3. **Place the phone at eye level**, held upright and level, several feet away. Your helper should not hold it above or below your face.
4. **Frame head and shoulders** with space above your head and on both sides. Do not zoom in so far that your face fills the screen; leave room to crop.
5. **Tap your face to focus** and lock exposure, so the white wall does not make your face look dark.
6. **Take several photos**, looking straight at the lens with a neutral expression, both eyes open and your mouth closed.
7. **Check each photo at full size** for sharpness, shadows and glare, and keep the best one without editing it.
8. **Crop it to size**, or send the original file to your computer for cropping and printing.

## Apps and tools to crop and check

The photo from your phone still needs to be cropped to the passport size: 2 × 2 inches for a US passport, with the head 1 to 1 3/8 inches (25–35 mm) from chin to top of hair. You can crop it yourself, or use an app or website; our [comparison of online passport photo websites](/tool/best-online-passport-photo-services) sets out what each one offers.

For a US passport, choose a tool that only crops and sizes the photo. Apps that smooth skin, reshape faces or replace the background change the photo, and the State Department checks for that, including changes made with AI. The [online photo maker](/tool/online-passport-photo-maker) uses crop-only mode for US passports: it measures and crops your photo and leaves the background as you took it.

When you move the file, use the original. Sending a photo by text message or some chat apps compresses it, and the State Department advises against texting the image. AirDrop, a cable, email as an attachment or a cloud folder keep the full quality.

## Common phone-photo problems

### Distortion

The face looks wider in the middle, or the nose looks too big. The phone was too close, or the ultra-wide lens was used. Move the phone a few feet away, use the 1x or 2x lens, and crop afterwards.

### Shadows and glare

Shadows on one side of the face, under the chin or on the wall behind you come from a single light source. Face a window, add a second light, or step a little away from the wall. Glare on the forehead or nose usually comes from flash or a lamp too close: turn the flash off and soften the light.

### Filters and beauty mode

Many phones smooth skin or adjust colour automatically, especially on the front camera. Turn these features off before the photo, not afterwards: a photo that has been smoothed, filtered or had its background blurred counts as edited, even if the phone did it for you.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I use an iPhone photo for my passport?

Yes. An iPhone photo is accepted if it meets every rule: the right size, a plain white or off-white background, no shadows, and no filters, Portrait mode or other edits.

### Can I take a selfie for a passport photo?

It is better not to. A selfie at arm's length distorts the face and is often tilted. Use a tripod and the timer, or ask someone to take it.

### Which camera lens should I use?

The main 1x lens or a 2x zoom from a few feet away. Avoid the 0.5x ultra-wide lens, which stretches the face.

### Can I renew my passport online with a phone photo?

Yes. The online renewal system accepts the formats most phones use, including JPG and HEIC. Upload the original file, taken in the last six months and not sent by text message.
